**Base-modification detection using Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT)**

Oxford Nanopore sequencing enables direct analysis of DNA modifications by monitoring alterations in ionic current as native DNA molecules traverse a nanopore. Because modified nucleotides produce characteristic signal shifts, epigenetic marks can be identified without the chemical conversion, enrichment, or antibody-based procedures required by many conventional methods. Available computational models currently support detection of several DNA modifications, including 5-methylcytosine (5mC), 5-hydroxymethylcytosine (5hmC), N6-methyladenine (6mA), and 4-methylcytosine (4mC).

For virology applications, a major advantage of this approach is that modification information remains linked to individual long sequencing reads. This allows epigenetic features to be examined in the context of complete viral haplotypes, sequence variation, and genomic regions that are repetitive or otherwise difficult to analyse using short-read technologies. However, modification calling relies heavily on the availability and quality of trained signal models, meaning performance can vary among different modification classes and genomic sequence contexts.

Preservation of native DNA is essential for accurate modification analysis. Library preparation strategies involving PCR amplification erase the original modification landscape because newly synthesised DNA lacks the native epigenetic marks. Consequently, amplification-free workflows are generally preferred when modification detection is a primary objective. For viral genome studies, it is also advisable to compare modification calls against appropriate controls, such as unmethylated reference material, particularly when the viral epigenetic state may differ substantially from the datasets used to train existing detection models.


**DNA-modification detection using PacBio SMRT sequencing**

PacBio single-molecule real-time (SMRT) sequencing can identify DNA modifications by exploiting their effects on DNA polymerase kinetics during sequencing. Modified nucleotides influence the rate at which the polymerase incorporates bases, producing measurable changes in the interval between successive fluorescent signals, referred to as the interpulse duration (IPD). By analysing these kinetic signatures, DNA modifications can be inferred directly from native molecules without the need for bisulfite conversion, enzymatic processing, or affinity-based enrichment.

Several common modifications generate characteristic kinetic patterns, including N6-methyladenine (6mA), 5-methylcytosine (5mC), and 5-hydroxymethylcytosine (5hmC). Detection sensitivity is not equivalent across all modification types, however, with 5mC generally producing a weaker signal than 6mA. These capabilities are particularly relevant to herpesvirus research, where viral genome methylation can vary according to the stage of infection and associated chromatin environment.

SMRT-based modification analysis offers two important advantages. First, modification calls are assigned at the level of individual DNA molecules, allowing heterogeneous populations of viral genomes to be characterised directly rather than being represented as a single averaged signal. Second, because these measurements are obtained from long reads, modification patterns can be assessed within repetitive or structurally complex genomic regions that are often problematic for short-read methylation assays.

The utility of this approach depends on several experimental considerations. As with nanopore-based modification detection, amplification-free library preparation is required because PCR removes native epigenetic information. In addition, modification-calling performance on viral genomes can benefit from calibration against suitable unmethylated controls, since most available prediction frameworks were originally developed using host genomic DNA rather than virus-specific training datasets.
