**Base-modification detection by ONT**

For nanopore DNA sequencing approaches, nucleotide modifications are directly detected from changes in the electrical current generated as native DNA passes through the nanopore, allowing modified bases to be identified without bisulfite conversion or other chemical enrichment. Current models support several modifications, including 5-methylcytosine (5mC), 5-hydroxymethylcytosine (5hmC), N6-methyladenine (6mA) and 4-methylcytosine (4mC). This is particularly useful for viral genomes because modification calls are retained on individual long DNA molecules, allowing modification patterns to be resolved alongside sequence variation and across repetitive or structurally complex regions. The principal limitation is that modification detection is model-dependent and modification-specific, with performance varying according to the modification and sequence context. Crucially, the DNA must remain native: PCR-based library preparation removes the original modification state, whereas amplification-free workflows preserve it. For viral genomes, modification calls should therefore ideally be validated against an appropriate unmethylated or otherwise defined control, particularly where viral modification patterns differ from those represented in the training data. 

**DNA-modification detection by PacBio**

Base modifications in viral DNA genomes alter the polymerase's incorporation kinetics, briefly stalling or delaying nucleotide addition during PacBio single-molecule real-time (SMRT) sequencing. By measuring the duration of these interpulse durations (IPD, the time interval between successive fluorescent pulse detections) SMRT sequencing identifies epigenetic modifications directly from native reads without requiring bisulfite conversion, enzymatic treatment, or antibody enrichment. N6-methyladenine, 5-methylcytosine, and 5-hydroxymethylcytosine produce distinguishable kinetic signatures, although the signal for 5-methylcytosine is considerably weaker than for N6-methyladenine (60). This is of direct interest in herpesvirology, where methylation of the viral genome differs between lytic and latent infection. This capability provides two main analytical advantages. First, because modification calls are assigned to individual molecules, a sample containing viral genomes in different chromatin states returns the distribution of those states rather than their average. Second, because these calls sit on long reads, they extend into repetitive regions where short-read bisulfite mapping is unreliable. Two conditions limit these advantages, however: the workflow must be amplification-free, since PCR replaces modified bases with unmodified ones; and calls on viral genomes should be calibrated against an unmethylated control, because the available models were trained on host DNA.
