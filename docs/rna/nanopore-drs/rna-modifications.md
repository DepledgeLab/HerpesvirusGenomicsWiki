Methods for detecting RNA modifications -- including m6A, m5C, inosine, pseudouridine, and 2’O-methylation -- have improved substantially, including highly sensitive modification-specific methods such as short-read GLORI-Seq (m6A) and BACS (pseudouridine), as well as direct detection of modifications through nanopore DRS. While these approaches offer new opportunities to study RNA regulation, they remain limited by the lack of robust ground-truth datasets and standardized analytical guidelines. Readers should be aware that multiple recent benchmarking studies have determined that Dorado-based RNA modification calling is prone to high false-positive rates. These studies have generally concluded that extensive calibration, filtering, and validation by orthologous methods is absolutely essential. This should include the use of in vitro transcribed (i.e. RNA modification-free) controls, as well as the use of RNA modification installation inhibitors where possible.  

For nanopore DNA sequencing approaches, nucleotide modifications are directly detected from changes in the electrical current generated as native DNA passes through the nanopore, allowing modified bases to be identified without bisulfite conversion or other chemical enrichment. Current models support several modifications, including 5-methylcytosine (5mC), 5-hydroxymethylcytosine (5hmC), N6-methyladenine (6mA) and 4-methylcytosine (4mC). This is particularly useful for viral genomes because modification calls are retained on individual long DNA molecules, allowing modification patterns to be resolved alongside sequence variation and across repetitive or structurally complex regions. The principal limitation is that modification detection is model-dependent and modification-specific, with performance varying according to the modification and sequence context. Crucially, the DNA must remain native: PCR-based library preparation removes the original modification state, whereas amplification-free workflows preserve it. For viral genomes, modification calls should therefore ideally be validated against an appropriate unmethylated or otherwise defined control, particularly where viral modification patterns differ from those represented in the training data.
